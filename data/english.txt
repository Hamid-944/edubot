ENGLISH – School Study Material

=== PARTS OF SPEECH ===
Every word in English belongs to a category called a part of speech. The eight main parts of speech are nouns, pronouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, prepositions, conjunctions, and interjections.

A noun is a word that names a person, place, thing, or idea. Examples: teacher, school, book, happiness, London, river. There are two types of nouns: common nouns (general names like "city," "dog," "mountain") and proper nouns (specific names like "Paris," "Buddy," "Mount Everest"). Proper nouns always begin with a capital letter. Collective nouns name a group, such as "a flock of birds," "a team of players," or "a pack of wolves." Abstract nouns name things we cannot see or touch, like love, fear, joy, freedom, and knowledge.

A pronoun is a word used in place of a noun to avoid repetition. Personal pronouns include: I, you, he, she, it, we, they (subject pronouns) and me, you, him, her, it, us, them (object pronouns). Possessive pronouns show ownership: mine, yours, his, hers, its, ours, theirs.

A verb is a word that expresses an action or a state of being. Action verbs show what someone or something does: run, jump, eat, sing, think, write. Linking verbs connect the subject to a description: am, is, are, was, were, seem, become, appear, feel, look. Helping (auxiliary) verbs work with main verbs: has, have, had, will, would, can, could, should, must, do, does, did.

An adjective is a word that describes or modifies a noun. It answers the questions "What kind?", "How many?", or "Which one?" Examples: tall, beautiful, three, red, small, happy, enormous. Articles (a, an, the) are a special type of adjective. Use "a" before consonant sounds (a cat, a dog) and "an" before vowel sounds (an apple, an hour).

An adverb modifies a verb, adjective, or another adverb. It answers questions like "How?", "When?", "Where?", and "How much?" Many adverbs end in -ly: quickly, slowly, carefully, loudly. Other adverbs: always, never, here, there, very, quite, already, soon.

=== VERB TENSES ===
Tense tells us when an action takes place. The three main tenses are past, present, and future.

Present tense describes actions happening now or regularly. Simple present: "I walk to school." Present continuous (progressive): "I am walking to school" (action happening right now). Present perfect: "I have walked to school" (action completed at some point before now).

Past tense describes actions that already happened. Simple past: "I walked to school." Past continuous: "I was walking to school" (action was in progress in the past). Past perfect: "I had walked to school before the rain started" (action completed before another past action).

Future tense describes actions that will happen. Simple future: "I will walk to school." Future with going to: "I am going to walk to school" (planned action). Future continuous: "I will be walking to school at 8 AM."

Regular verbs form the past tense by adding -ed: walk → walked, jump → jumped, play → played. Irregular verbs change their form in the past tense: go → went, eat → ate, write → wrote, see → saw, take → took, run → ran, speak → spoke.

=== SENTENCE STRUCTURE ===
A sentence is a group of words that expresses a complete thought. Every sentence needs a subject (who or what the sentence is about) and a predicate (what the subject does or is). Example: "The dog (subject) barks loudly (predicate)."

There are four types of sentences: declarative (makes a statement: "The sky is blue."), interrogative (asks a question: "Is the sky blue?"), imperative (gives a command: "Look at the sky."), and exclamatory (expresses strong emotion: "What a beautiful sky!").

A simple sentence has one independent clause: "She sings beautifully." A compound sentence joins two independent clauses with a coordinating conjunction (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so — remembered as FANBOYS): "She sings beautifully, and he plays guitar." A complex sentence has one independent clause and one or more dependent clauses: "Although it was raining, she went for a walk."

=== PUNCTUATION ===
A period (.) ends a declarative or imperative sentence. A question mark (?) ends an interrogative sentence. An exclamation mark (!) ends an exclamatory sentence or shows strong emphasis.

A comma (,) separates items in a list (I bought apples, oranges, and bananas), separates two independent clauses joined by a conjunction (I wanted to go, but I was tired), and sets off introductory words or phrases (However, I disagree. After the match, we celebrated).

An apostrophe (') is used for contractions (can't = cannot, it's = it is, they're = they are) and to show possession (John's book, the teacher's pen, the students' classroom for plural possession).

Quotation marks (" ") enclose direct speech: She said, "I will be there at noon." A colon (:) introduces a list or explanation. A semicolon (;) connects two closely related independent clauses.

=== READING COMPREHENSION ===
Reading comprehension means understanding what you read. When reading a text, look for the main idea, which is the most important point the author wants to communicate. Supporting details provide evidence or examples for the main idea.

There are different types of questions you may encounter: literal questions (answers are directly stated in the text), inferential questions (require you to "read between the lines" and use context clues), and evaluative questions (require you to judge or give your opinion).

Useful comprehension strategies include: previewing headings and pictures before reading, identifying unfamiliar vocabulary using context clues, making predictions about what will happen next, visualizing (creating mental images of what you read), summarizing (retelling the main points in your own words), and making connections between the text and your own experience.

=== ESSAY AND PARAGRAPH WRITING ===
A paragraph is a group of sentences about one main idea. A well-structured paragraph has three parts: a topic sentence (states the main idea), supporting sentences (provide details, examples, or evidence), and a concluding sentence (wraps up the paragraph).

An essay is a piece of writing that discusses a topic. A standard five-paragraph essay has an introduction, three body paragraphs, and a conclusion. The introduction begins with a hook (an interesting opening sentence), provides background information, and ends with a thesis statement (the main argument or purpose of the essay).

Each body paragraph focuses on one main point that supports the thesis. Use transition words and phrases to connect ideas smoothly: first, furthermore, in addition, however, on the other hand, as a result, in conclusion, therefore, finally.

The conclusion restates the thesis in different words, summarizes the main points, and ends with a closing thought or call to action. Avoid introducing new information in the conclusion.

Good writing uses varied sentence structures, strong vocabulary, and clear organization. Always proofread your work for spelling, grammar, and punctuation errors.

=== LITERARY DEVICES ===
Authors use literary devices to make writing more vivid and interesting. A simile compares two unlike things using "like" or "as": "Her voice was as smooth as silk." A metaphor compares two unlike things without using "like" or "as": "Life is a journey." Personification gives human qualities to non-human things: "The wind whispered through the trees."

Alliteration is the repetition of the same beginning consonant sound: "Peter Piper picked a peck of pickled peppers." Onomatopoeia is a word that imitates the sound it describes: buzz, hiss, splash, crash, boom. Hyperbole is an exaggeration used for effect: "I have told you a million times!" Irony occurs when the meaning is opposite to what is said.

The setting is the time and place where a story takes place. The theme is the central message or lesson of a story. The plot is the sequence of events in a story, typically following the pattern: exposition (introduction), rising action, climax (turning point), falling action, and resolution.
